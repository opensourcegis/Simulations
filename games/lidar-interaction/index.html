INTERPRETATION Beyond the last return

Parts of the illuminated footprint can pass through gaps in foliage and reach lower surfaces. The first detected return is the nearest detectable contribution, not necessarily the tree apex. The last return may be ground, lower foliage or an obstruction. Build a DTM from classified ground and a surface model on a common reference before estimating canopy height; subtracting two arbitrary returns is not sufficient.

What happens when the pulse hits something

The basic balance is incident = reflected + absorbed + transmitted. We split reflection into diffuse and specular components; scattering is not a separate channel in addition to all reflected energy:

Ei = Er,diffuse + Er,specular + Ea + Et
InteractionWhat it doesDoes the receiver see it?
Diffuse reflectionA rough surface redistributes reflected energy. An ideal Lambertian reflector has angle-independent radiance; power per solid angle has cosine geometry.The small backscattered share entering the aperture can be detected.
Specular reflectionA smooth surface reflects around the mirror direction, with θr = θi.Strong when the specular lobe overlaps the receiver, often near normal incidence for a monostatic sensor.
AbsorptionEnergy is converted to heat within the material.No — it is lost.
TransmissionEnergy passes through (glass, gaps in a canopy, water column).No, but it may meet something deeper and return later.

Both reflection components can produce a return. The receiver collects only the portion directed into its aperture. Echo delay estimates range; an intensity value depends on received energy, receiver response, gain and processing. It is not automatically calibrated reflectance.

Changing the angle changes everything. Swing the incident beam away from the normal and two things can happen: the specular component grows toward glancing incidence, but its lobe moves away from the receiver; and the diffuse backscatter changes with cosine geometry. Real BRDFs depend on roughness, wavelength and material.

This lesson uses illustrative 1064 nm coefficients. Topographic sensors use several near-infrared wavelengths; bathymetric instruments commonly use green light. Visible colour alone does not specify reflectance at a LiDAR wavelength. Transmitted rays are schematic: refraction, thickness and internal reflections are not modelled.

Canopy model: 21 weighted sub-rays sample a truncated Gaussian beam in this 2D cross-section. Foliage clusters are effective attenuating volumes, not solid leaves. Transmission follows T = exp(−∫k dl); echoes are attenuated on both outgoing and return paths, so ground backscatter scales with T². The trunk is opaque. Foliage backscatter is integrated in 0.1 m layers. Overlapping clusters add extinction. Hollow markers show contribution centroids and filled markers show detector peaks, not individual leaves.

Receiver model: pulse energy stays fixed when width changes. Additive Gaussian electronics/background noise is sampled per shot; the detector uses a short moving average, threshold max(0.02, 5 × raw noise RMS), and half-FWHM peak suppression. This is not a manufacturer's signal processor. Photon shot noise, multiple scattering, atmospheric turbulence and a full 3D canopy are omitted. Ground labels use known scene geometry, not an operational classifier.
