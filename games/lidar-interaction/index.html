Why this matters

A camera only ever sees the top of the canopy. A LiDAR pulse is wider than the gaps between leaves, so part of it slips through and keeps going. The first return maps the canopy surface (DSM), the last return — if enough energy survives — maps the bare earth (DTM). Subtract them and you get canopy height.

What happens when the pulse hits something

Every laser pulse that leaves the scanner carries energy Ei. When it strikes a target that energy splits four ways, and it always balances:

Ei = Er (reflected) + Ea (absorbed) + Et (transmitted) + Es (scattered)
InteractionWhat it doesDoes the receiver see it?
ScatteringA rough surface disperses energy in many directions, over the whole hemisphere.Yes — this is the return we measure.
ReflectionA smooth surface bounces energy back in a single direction, with θr = θi.Only when that direction points back at the sensor — i.e. at normal incidence.
AbsorptionEnergy is converted to heat within the material.No — it is lost.
TransmissionEnergy passes through (glass, gaps in a canopy, water column).No, but it may meet something deeper and return later.

Only Scattering brings the pulse home. Even then the receiver aperture covers a minuscule slice of that hemisphere — typically well under one part per million. The sensor times that faint echo to get range, R = c·Δt/2, and records its amplitude as intensity.

Changing the angle changes everything. Swing the incident beam away from the normal and two things happen at once: Reflection grows (a surface becomes more mirror-like at a glancing angle) but it leaves at θr = θi, further and further from the receiver; and Scattering falls off as cos θi. That is why a lake blazes straight beneath the aircraft and vanishes a few degrees to either side.

Reflectance is quoted at the sensor wavelength — most topographic LiDAR uses 1064 nm (near infra-red). That is why vegetation looks bright (leaves reflect NIR strongly) while water looks black (water absorbs NIR almost completely).
