Aerial Triangulation Lab

Your survey aircraft flew one strip and took three overlapping photos (60% forward overlap). The navigation system recorded only rough camera positions and attitudes — good enough to start with, far too poor to make a map from. Aerial triangulation (AT) is how photogrammetrists fix that: measure points connecting the photos, then let a bundle adjustment solve for the true camera positions and orientations.

Your job as the AT operator

  1. Measure tie points. Pick a sharp feature (building corner, road intersection, lone tree) and click it in every photo where it appears. Each completed point ties the photos together with light rays.
  2. Follow the von Gruber pattern. Spread points over the top, middle and bottom of each stereo overlap — the coverage grid tracks this. Points in the triple overlap (seen in all 3 photos) are the strongest.
  3. Ground control is done for you. The three green ▲ targets are signalized ground control points (GCPs) with surveyed coordinates, already measured in the images. They anchor the block to the ground.
  4. Run the bundle adjustment. Watch the camera estimates and image footprints being pulled into place as the least-squares solution converges.

How you are scored

  • Tie point accuracy — do your clicked rays truly intersect? (ray residual in metres)
  • Distribution — all six von Gruber zones covered?
  • Redundancy — 3-ray points in the triple overlap
  • Checkpoint QA — after adjustment, four hidden surveyed checkpoints are computed from your solution and compared to truth, exactly like production AT quality control.
Tips: a magnifier loupe follows your cursor for precise clicking. Click a marker to re-select its tie point; click again elsewhere to move the measurement. Mismatched points (clicking different features) become blunders that drag the whole block — exactly as in real AT.
