From tilted photograph to map

An aerial photo is a central perspective taken through one lens point, and it is never perfectly vertical — there is always a little tilt (ω, φ). Two separate distortions follow:

  • Tilt / perspective. Because the photo plane is not parallel to the ground, a square on the ground images as a keystoned quadrilateral. That is why the 50 m grid converges and why roofs are not rectangles in the photo.
  • Relief displacement. Anything above the datum is imaged further from the nadir than its true map position, by d = r·h/H.

Rectification is an inverse problem

For every cell of the output map we ask “what colour belongs at (X, Y)?”. We take the elevation Z at that cell from the elevation model, then the collinearity equations tell us exactly which image pixel saw (X, Y, Z). Both the tilt and the relief are undone at once — provided the elevation surface is right:

  • Datum — Z = 0. Tilt is removed, relief displacement is not.
  • DTM — terrain corrected. Buildings are absent from a DTM, so a roof pixel is projected down to the ground beside the building: roofs stay displaced, façades stay smeared. The classic conventional orthophoto.
  • DSM — the roof cell carries the roof’s own Z, so each roof returns to its footprint as a true rectangle. A façade is vertical: it owns zero map area, so it disappears. This is the true orthophoto.

Occlusion

The camera never saw ground hidden behind a tall building. A visibility test masks those cells; without it, façade pixels are copied into the gap and the building ghosts. Production pipelines fill from neighbouring overlapping photos that did see that ground — which is why true ortho demands high overlap.
