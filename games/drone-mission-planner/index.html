Mission briefing

You are the pilot. Plan a photogrammetry survey that actually flies.

Every control starts at zero or at the bottom of its range. Nothing here is set for you — the plan on the screen right now is a legal flight that would waste a battery and deliver nothing. Build it up yourself.

  1. 1
    Pick a takeoff site Tap a pad on the map. The trees around each pad show the clearing you actually get: a wide opening means room to abort and somewhere to put down, a tight one means neither. Sitting further from the block also costs you the transit.
  2. 2
    Point the headings at the wind Drag the two compass dials. Take off into the wind for lift and control. Fly your survey legs across the wind so the long passes stay straight.
  3. 3
    Fly the altitude the brief asks for The survey is commissioned at 2.5–3.0 cm/px, which puts you between 100 m and 120 m. Lower is not better — it is just a longer flight for detail nobody ordered. Watch the towers, though: you clear everything you fly over by 20 m, and the tall masts push that floor up towards the top of the window.
  4. 4
    Find the overlap that works Side and forward overlap both start at zero and nobody is going to tell you where to put them. Move one, watch the score, put it back. Too little tears the model apart; too much costs flight time and photos and buys nothing.
  5. 5
    Mind the ground, then submit The drone holds height above ground, so it climbs over every hill on the block and the battery pays for it. Watch the contour rings and the climb readout. The camera also needs 2 seconds between RAW frames, and you must land with 30% in hand.

The five terms that matter

GSD — ground sample distance
How much ground one pixel covers. 3 cm/px means each pixel is 3 cm across. On this camera it is simply altitude ÷ 40: 120 m gives you 3 cm, 100 m gives you 2.5 cm.
Side overlap
How much neighbouring strips overlap side-to-side. Higher overlap packs lanes closer together, so the flight gets longer and you cross the hills more often.
Forward overlap
How much consecutive photos along a lane overlap. Higher overlap means photos are taken closer together, so the camera has to fire faster.
Trigger interval
Seconds between shots. Shooting RAW, the camera needs about two seconds to clear its buffer. Below that you drop frames and the survey has holes in it.
Terrain climb
The vertical the route flies because the ground underneath it rises and falls. It never shows up in the route length, but it comes straight out of the battery.
